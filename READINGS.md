# Connection Without Losing Yourself

Practice asking, listening and setting limits while keeping both people’s choices visible. Eight readings explore closeness that can make room for independence.

Book starting point: [The Balancing Act — Nedra Glover Tawwab (2026)](https://www.penguinrandomhouse.com/books/760457/the-balancing-act-by-nedra-glover-tawwab/).

Four connected paths through action, purpose, beliefs and relationships. These original guides use selected 2026 books as starting points alongside independent research. They are not book reproductions, author-endorsed editions or clinical assessments. Exercises are original invitations to reflect; no private answers are collected.

## Closeness and independence can share the same relationship

Nedra Glover Tawwab’s The Balancing Act provides the starting point for this collection’s exploration of connection and independence. The readings and exercises here are original, with additional research linked where relevant. They are not a substitute for the book or an assessment of a particular relationship.

Balance need not mean identical contributions in every moment. During illness, a move or a demanding week, one person may provide more support. The question is whether both people’s needs and choices remain visible over time, and whether the arrangement can be discussed.

Begin with one ordinary interaction rather than judging the whole relationship. Perhaps a recurring phone call often happens at a difficult time, or shared plans leave little room for rest. A specific situation makes it easier to consider a workable change without turning either person into the problem.

### Try this — original exercise

Choose one recurring interaction. Describe what works, what feels difficult and one change you would like to discuss. Keep the description specific enough that another person could recognize the situation.

## Name the need before negotiating the solution

A proposed solution can be easier to discuss when the need beneath it is clear. “We must cancel every Friday plan” names one answer. “I need some recovery time after work” opens several possibilities: meeting later, alternating weeks or choosing a quieter activity.

Use an observation that can be checked. “We have gone out after work for the past three Fridays” gives the conversation firmer ground than “You never let me rest.” Clear wording does not guarantee agreement, and it cannot solve every conflict. It can make the actual issue easier to understand.

This is an original communication exercise inspired by the book’s theme. It leaves room for a different solution from the one you first imagined. Another person may have a need of their own, and the practical question becomes whether an arrangement can accommodate both without pretending that every preference is equally easy to meet.

### Try this — original exercise

Write an observation, the need you associate with it and two possible solutions. Mark which parts are essential and which are preferences you could discuss.

- [The Balancing Act — Nedra Glover Tawwab (2026)](https://www.penguinrandomhouse.com/books/760457/the-balancing-act-by-nedra-glover-tawwab/) — Tarcher

## Make a request that can receive an honest answer

A request provides enough detail for another person to respond. It names an action and a time, and it allows a genuine answer. “Could you review these two paragraphs by Thursday?” is clearer than “I need you to be more supportive.”

The research on autonomy provides background for respecting choice; it does not validate this exact wording as a universal technique. A request becomes difficult to answer honestly when a no triggers an unrelated punishment or when the real expectation is hidden.

Some situations involve agreed responsibilities, not optional favors. Name those responsibilities plainly instead of disguising them as requests. If a shared task cannot be completed as planned, discuss the constraint and what needs to happen next. Respecting another person’s choice can coexist with taking commitments seriously and deciding what you are able to rely on.

### Try this — original exercise

Draft a request with an action, time frame and reason. Write how you would respond to yes, no or a suggested alternative. Check whether you are actually discussing an existing obligation.

- [Self-Determination Theory and the Facilitation of Intrinsic Motivation, Social Development, and Well-Being](https://selfdeterminationtheory.org/SDT/documents/2000_RyanDeci_SDT.pdf) — Ryan & Deci · American Psychologist, 2000

## A limit describes what you can participate in

A workable limit describes your availability or participation. “I can discuss this for twenty minutes tonight” gives information about what you can offer. It does not require the other person to feel pleased about the limit or agree with your reason.

Choose follow-through that is realistic and consistent with your responsibilities. Changing an arrangement may require notice, a handover or a discussion of shared commitments. A limit is less useful when it is announced dramatically but cannot be maintained.

This reflection concerns ordinary interactions where a conversation is reasonably safe. If stating a limit could provoke threats or coercion, tailored support and safety planning matter more than finding the perfect sentence. For an everyday scheduling problem, however, a short explanation and a clear alternative may be enough. You can care about someone’s disappointment without treating it as proof that your availability must be unlimited.

### Try this — original exercise

Write one limit in terms of your time, energy or participation. Add any notice or practical arrangement it requires. Remove instructions about what the other person must feel.

- [The Balancing Act — Nedra Glover Tawwab (2026)](https://www.penguinrandomhouse.com/books/760457/the-balancing-act-by-nedra-glover-tawwab/) — Tarcher

## Listening includes checking whether you understood

Research on intimacy by Jean-Philippe Laurenceau and colleagues examined disclosure and perceived partner responsiveness in daily interactions. It offers a reason to pay attention to whether a person feels understood, while not proving that a particular listening script will improve every relationship.

Try reflecting the main point in your own words and inviting correction: “It sounds as though the last-minute change was the hardest part. Have I understood?” Then allow the correction to matter. Listening becomes less useful when the summary is merely a pause before returning to your own argument.

Understanding does not require agreement. You can recognize why an event mattered to someone and still have a different account or limit. Before offering advice, ask whether they want help thinking through options or simply a chance to explain. The answer may change as the conversation develops.

### Try this — original exercise

In a willing conversation, summarize one point and invite correction. Ask whether the person wants listening, ideas or practical help. Notice what you learn before deciding what to say next.

- [Intimacy as an interpersonal process: self-disclosure and perceived partner responsiveness](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9599440/) — Laurenceau, Barrett & Pietromonaco · Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 1998

## Repair a specific moment instead of winning the whole argument

A repair can begin with a particular action: “I interrupted you twice while you were explaining the plan.” That is more concrete than declaring yourself a terrible person or insisting that the entire disagreement was someone else’s fault. It gives both people something identifiable to discuss.

This is an original exercise, not a universally validated apology formula. Research on responsiveness and self-compassion provides adjacent context; neither guarantees reconciliation. The other person may understand the event differently or need time before responding.

Describe the effect as something you observed or ask about it rather than assuming you know. Then propose a change you can actually make. A promise to pause before responding is more useful when it becomes visible in later conversations. Repair depends on behavior over time, not just the quality of a sentence, and accepting responsibility does not require agreeing to every accusation.

### Try this — original exercise

Write the action you want to acknowledge, what you know or need to ask about its effect, and one realistic repair. Include how you will follow through without demanding immediate forgiveness.

- [Intimacy as an interpersonal process: self-disclosure and perceived partner responsiveness](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9599440/) — Laurenceau, Barrett & Pietromonaco · Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 1998
- [Self-compassion increases self-improvement motivation](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22645164/) — Breines & Chen · Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin, 2012

## Share support across a wider circle

One person may be central to your life without being the right companion for every interest, difficulty or practical need. A wider circle can include a friend who enjoys walking, a professional who answers specialist questions and a community where a shared interest makes conversation easier.

Research about everyday social ties is relevant background, not proof that everyone needs a large network. Access and preferences differ. The aim is to notice possible settings for connection without assigning people jobs they have not agreed to perform.

Consider what you are asking from one relationship and whether another willing person or appropriate service could meet part of the need. Respect privacy when sharing information about others. Expanding support does not have to mean replacing a valued relationship; it can simply make room for different kinds of contact and for each person to have a life beyond a single bond.

### Try this — original exercise

Name one kind of support you currently seek from just one person. Identify another appropriate setting or resource, then choose a respectful way to explore it without assuming anyone owes you a role.

- [Social Interactions and Well-Being: The Surprising Power of Weak Ties](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24769739/) — Sandstrom & Dunn · Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin, 2014

## Knowledge floor: what does balance look like here?

No book or general study can prescribe the fairest arrangement for a particular pair of people without knowing their circumstances. Even a thoughtful conversation may leave an important difference unresolved. One person cannot create mutual cooperation alone.

Where both people are willing, try a specific agreement with a review date. For example, choose when to discuss shared plans, how to give notice of a change and what each person is responsible for. Keep the agreement small enough that you can tell whether it happened.

At the review, distinguish the intention from the behavior. Did the arrangement occur? Was it workable for both people? What changed around it? This is personal learning, not a test that proves a relationship is healthy or unhealthy. The next step may be an adjustment, a different kind of support or a clearer decision about what you can participate in.

### Try this — original exercise

Propose one voluntary agreement and a date to revisit it. Define what you will observe and what each person can change. Leave room for an honest conclusion if the arrangement does not work.
